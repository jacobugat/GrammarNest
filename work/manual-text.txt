[Heading 1] Antes de empezar: separables e inseparables
[Normal] Un phrasal verb es un verbo con una partícula (up, off, in, out…) que juntos forman un significado nuevo: get up = levantarse. Algunos aceptan el objeto en medio y otros no. Por eso cada phrasal verb de este libro lleva un símbolo que te dice de cuál tipo es:
[TABLA]
Símbolo | Tipo | Cómo funciona
↔ | Separable | El objeto puede ir en medio o después de la partícula. / turn off the light  =  turn the light off / Si el objeto es un pronombre (it, them, him, her, me, you, us), va siempre en medio. Correcto: turn it off. Incorrecto: turn off it.
 | Inseparable | El verbo y la partícula nunca se separan; el objeto va después. / look after my brother  →  look after him / Correcto: look after him. Incorrecto: look him after. / Aquí entran también los que no llevan objeto (get up, hang out) y los de tres palabras (come up with). Algunos, como look after o believe in, son técnicamente verbos preposicionales; en este libro se estudian igual.
[/TABLA]
[Heading 2] Cómo recordarlo
[List Paragraph] Pregúntate: ¿puedo meter el objeto en medio? Si puedes, es ↔. Si no, es .
[List Paragraph] Con los ↔, si el objeto es un pronombre, ponlo siempre en medio: pick it up, call it off.
[List Paragraph] Si un verbo tiene un uso con objeto y otro sin objeto (take off: «quitarse algo» y «despegar»), el símbolo indica el uso con objeto. En los demás casos corresponde al significado que trae el libro.
[List Paragraph] Verás el símbolo junto a cada phrasal verb en las tablas de expresiones nuevas, en la Fase 5, en el seguimiento y en la clave, y la leyenda está en el encabezado de cada página. En las recuperaciones no aparece: ahí lo recuerdas tú.
[Heading 1] 1. Cómo funciona este libro
[Normal] Este libro no es un diccionario. Cada expresión aparece varias veces, en páginas distintas y con actividades distintas, para que pases de reconocerla a recuperarla y usarla de forma espontánea. La repetición no consiste en copiar o leer lo mismo muchas veces, sino en recordar la expresión mediante tareas variadas.
[Heading 2] Objetivo
[Normal] Facilitar la retención a largo plazo y la producción espontánea mediante repetición espaciada, recuperación activa, asociación multimodal y transferencia de las expresiones a contextos personales y comunicativos.
[Heading 2] Principios
[List Paragraph] Aprendizaje contextualizado: cada expresión llega con una frase, una situación y una asociación, no como una traducción aislada.
[List Paragraph] Repetición espaciada: una expresión reaparece después de intervalos crecientes, mezclada con vocabulario nuevo y con una actividad diferente.
[List Paragraph] Recuperación activa: primero intentas recordar y después compruebas la respuesta en la clave del final.
[List Paragraph] Asociación mediante movimiento y visualización: gestos inspirados en TPR y escenas mentales. Son apoyos opcionales, no obligatorios.
[List Paragraph] Elaboración personal y transferencia: escribes tus propias oraciones, preguntas y situaciones.
[List Paragraph] Retirada progresiva de las ayudas: cada vez hay menos pistas, traducciones, gestos y modelos.

[Heading 1] 2. Cómo usar las sesiones
[List Paragraph] Haz una sesión por día o por sesión de estudio. Cada una tarda unos 20–30 minutos.
[List Paragraph] En cada recuperación, intenta responder antes de mirar la clave. Después compara y corrige.
[List Paragraph] Marca cómo te fue en cada ítem: sin pista, con pista o no recordé. Las expresiones que no recordaste deben ir al diario de errores.
[List Paragraph] Los gestos y las visualizaciones son opcionales, pero son más útiles cuando los haces en voz alta.
[List Paragraph] Las sesiones finales de consolidación no traen vocabulario nuevo: solo cierran las fases pendientes.
[List Paragraph] Después de cada cuatro unidades hay una lectura (después de las sesiones 5, 9, 13, 17 y 21) con los phrasal verbs de esas unidades. En cada espacio tienes el significado en español arriba y ninguna letra de pista: recuerda la expresión, conjúgala y escríbela. Al final del libro hay diez lecturas más para inspirar, con verbos de todas las unidades mezclados.
[List Paragraph] Este libro complementa la exposición real al idioma y la conversación; no los reemplaza.

[Heading 1] 3. Fundamentación teórica
[Normal] Este libro no parte de una sola teoría, sino de un conjunto de hallazgos de la investigación sobre adquisición de vocabulario en segunda lengua y de la psicología cognitiva de la memoria. Esta sección explica en qué se apoya cada decisión de diseño (la elección de las expresiones, la repetición espaciada, la recuperación activa, los gestos, las tareas de elaboración y la retirada de ayudas) y hasta dónde llega ese respaldo.
[Heading 2] Por qué phrasal verbs
[Normal] Los phrasal verbs (verbo + partícula) son muy frecuentes en el inglés hablado e informal y, a la vez, difíciles para quien aprende: existen miles y su significado no siempre se deduce de sus partes. Existen listas de los más frecuentes, pero Garnier y Schmitt (2015) advierten que no tienen en cuenta la polisemia: los más comunes tienen en promedio más de cinco sentidos, por lo que proponen priorizar sus sentidos más frecuentes. Siguiendo esa lógica, el libro trabaja expresiones de uso corriente y presenta cada una con un significado principal dentro de una frase y una situación concretas, no como una traducción aislada.
[Heading 2] Aprendizaje contextualizado e intencional
[Normal] Aprender vocabulario requiere encuentros repetidos y variados con cada expresión, tanto en el uso real del idioma como en el estudio deliberado (Nation, 2007; Schmitt, 2008). Nation (2007) propone que un buen programa equilibre cuatro líneas: input centrado en el significado, producción centrada en el significado, estudio centrado en la lengua y desarrollo de la fluidez. Este libro trabaja sobre todo la tercera, y sus lecturas y tareas de uso personal se acercan a las demás. Por eso se aclara que complementa la exposición real al idioma y la conversación, y no las reemplaza.
[Heading 2] Repetición espaciada
[Normal] Desde los trabajos de Ebbinghaus (1885/1913) se sabe que lo aprendido se olvida con rapidez si no se revisa. En una revisión cuantitativa de estudios con tareas de recuerdo verbal, Cepeda et al. (2006) encontraron que distribuir la práctica en el tiempo produce mejor retención que concentrarla, y que el intervalo ideal entre repasos depende de cuánto tiempo se quiere recordar el material. Dunlosky et al. (2013) valoran la práctica distribuida como una de las técnicas de estudio de mayor utilidad. En el libro, cada expresión reaparece en las sesiones n + 1, n + 2, n + 3, n + 5 y n + 8, mezclada con otras unidades. Estos intervalos concretos son una decisión de diseño inspirada en esos hallazgos, no una prescripción de la literatura.
[Heading 2] Recuperación activa
[Normal] Intentar recordar fortalece la memoria más que volver a leer. Roediger y Karpicke (2006) mostraron que hacer pruebas de recuerdo mejora la retención a largo plazo frente a releer el material, y Karpicke y Roediger (2008), trabajando con pares de palabras de otra lengua, concluyeron que la recuperación repetida es decisiva para el aprendizaje. De ahí la regla central del libro: primero intenta recordar y solo después consulta la clave. Bjork (1994) y Bjork y Bjork (2011) llaman «dificultades deseables» a las condiciones que hacen el estudio más esforzado en el momento, como espaciar y recuperar, pero lo vuelven más duradero.
[Heading 2] Asociación mediante movimiento y visualización
[Normal] La teoría de la codificación dual (Paivio, 1986) sostiene que la información se recuerda mejor cuando se representa de forma verbal y también como imagen. Los gestos inspirados en la Respuesta Física Total (TPR; Asher, 1969) y la visualización guiada buscan crear esa segunda representación. En el libro son apoyos opcionales porque la evidencia sobre su efecto específico en el vocabulario es más limitada que la del espaciado y la recuperación.
[Heading 2] Elaboración y transferencia
[Normal] Procesar el significado de una expresión con profundidad deja huellas más duraderas que procesarla de forma superficial (Craik & Lockhart, 1972). En vocabulario, la hipótesis de la carga de implicación (Hulstijn & Laufer, 2001) plantea que se retiene mejor cuando la tarea exige necesidad, búsqueda y evaluación, como ocurre al usar la expresión para producir un texto propio. Por eso la fase 5 pide oraciones, preguntas y situaciones personales en lugar de copiar el ejemplo.
[Heading 2] Retirada progresiva de las ayudas
[Normal] En las primeras fases el libro ofrece significado, ejemplo, gesto e iniciales; después solo el significado, y al final frases sin pistas. Esta secuencia se relaciona con la idea de andamiaje (Wood et al., 1976), vinculada a la zona de desarrollo próximo de Vygotsky (1978), y, por analogía, con el fading de Renkl y Atkinson (2003), que sustituye de manera gradual los ejemplos resueltos por problemas que el estudiante resuelve solo. Además, el efecto de inversión de la pericia (Kalyuga et al., 2003) advierte que las ayudas útiles para quien empieza pueden volverse innecesarias, o incluso perjudiciales, cuando ya domina el contenido. Por eso las expresiones difíciles deben reaparecer antes y las fáciles pueden revisarse después.

[Heading 2] Alcances y límites
[Normal] El modelo integra principios con respaldo empírico, pero la mayoría de esos estudios se hizo con materiales y contextos distintos a los de este libro. La eficacia del diseño completo (las seis fases, los intervalos y los niveles de dominio) debe comprobarse con estudiantes, que es el propósito de esta versión de prototipo.

[Normal] Lectura 1 · My First Week at La Gran Colombia
[Normal] My name is Camila, and I am a first-year student of Modern Languages in Bogotá. On my first Monday I got up at five because the trip to the university takes more than an hour. Since it was a special day, I tried on four shirts before I chose one. When the bus arrived, it was so full that I almost couldn't get in.
[Normal] In the classroom, the professor asked us to set up the chairs in a circle. It was warm, so I took off my jacket. The professor said she would deal with any question about the schedule, and when a student asked about the library, she promised to look into it. She also explained that she would take over the grammar class until the new teacher arrived.
[Normal] The first reading was long and difficult. I was afraid that I couldn't keep up with the group, but I told myself to press on. During the break I ran after a classmate and shouted, “Hang on!”
[Normal] That classmate was Daniel. He came up with a plan: study together every afternoon. He invited me to come along to the library, and we went through the first chapter line by line. I brought up my biggest problem, vocabulary, and Daniel brought out a notebook full of colors and drawings. “You should cut down on translations,” he said, “and never throw away your mistakes.” I decided not to put off my homework anymore. Daniel could not stay long because he has to look after his little sister in the afternoons, but my first week ended better than I expected.
[Normal] Lectura 2 · The Conversation Club
[Normal] In our second semester, our teacher asked us to take on a new project: an English conversation club for beginners. At first I wanted to give up because I had never taught before, so I asked for one night to think over the idea. The next day, my team and I met to carry out the plan. We get along very well, and that makes teamwork much easier. First, we needed to find out what the students wanted to learn, so we made a short survey, and we checked out several free apps for practice.
[Normal] On the first day we arrived early to tidy up the room. It was raining, so the students took off their wet coats at the door. One student, Laura, was so nervous that she almost cried. I tried to cheer up Laura with a joke and told her, “I believe in you.” Suddenly the floor was wet and a boy almost fell. “Watch out!” I shouted. Everyone laughed, and the class began.
[Normal] After the club, my teammate Sofia, who works out every morning, said, “Hurry up, or we'll miss lunch!” Sofia is a very kind person who cares for her grandmother at home. On weekends we hang out in a café near the historic center. Before our final presentation, a friend stayed over at my house so we could practice. In the morning I picked up some coffee on the way, and after the presentation we went back to the classroom and gave away our old books to the students.
[Normal] Lectura 3 · My Job at the Language Academy
[Normal] In my third year, I got a part-time job at a small language academy. The salary was low, so I had to get by carefully. I wanted to save up for a trip to Canada, so I decided to put aside a little money every week and cut back on snacks and taxis. I also had to pay back my cousin, who had lent me money for books, and later I managed to pay off a small loan.
[Normal] My first week was stressful. The projector broke down in the middle of a class, and a student started to panic. I told her to calm down. Then I tried to figure out the problem, but I couldn't, and my boss had to back me up. That same week the coordinator had to call off the evening class because of a storm, and a student who felt dizzy almost passed out in the hallway. After the storm, my brother picked me up at the academy. That night I had to do over my whole lesson plan, and I dived into grammar books to improve my explanations.
[Normal] The academy was also a happy place. Our colleague Mateo finally asked out the receptionist, and she said yes. On Fridays we met up with other teachers, and we caught up with old friends from the university. Sometimes a former classmate dropped by to say hello, and sometimes the teachers came over to my apartment for dinner.
[Normal] Lectura 4 · A Walking Tour of La Candelaria
[Normal] For our tourism practice, my group designed a walking tour of La Candelaria, the historic center of Bogotá. On Saturday morning we got together to practice. Our teacher told us to dress down because we would walk for hours, but she asked us to dress up a little for the final presentation. We got on the bus early and got off near the Plaza de Bolívar.
[Normal] The tourists arrived, and Daniel started the tour. The speaker was too quiet, so he decided to turn on the microphone and turn up the volume. At the Chorro de Quevedo, a tourist asked how the tradition of telling the legend of the city came about, and I explained it slowly. A street musician was playing very loudly, so we asked him to turn down his music for a minute. Then a man in the street butted in to tell his own version of the story, and everybody laughed.
[Normal] While we walked, I came across a small bookstore with old maps in the window. One tourist wanted to buy a souvenir, and I helped her pick out a handmade bag. Another tourist said, “Please slow down; I can't understand you.” But we only had two hours, so later we had to speed up to reach the Gold Museum. We had to put up with the cold wind and the rain, and Sofia was afraid to speak English in front of the crowd, but she did not chicken out. At the end, Daniel turned off the microphone.
[Normal] After the tour, some of us wanted to stay in because of the rain, but most of us decided to go out for hot chocolate with cheese. Finally, Sofia's father dropped us off at home. It was a long day, but we learned that a good guide needs language, culture and a lot of patience.
[Normal] Lectura 5 · A Weekend in Villa de Leyva
[Normal] At the end of the semester, our class took a weekend trip to Villa de Leyva. We arrived at the hostel at noon and checked in at the front desk. The receptionist spoke quickly, so we had to ask about the breakfast hours twice. I put my phone in the safe in our room, and later I took it out of there before dinner.
[Normal] That night we decided to eat out, so we looked for a restaurant in the main square and gobbled up a huge pizza. Then we played a game: each person had to make up a short story in English using five new words. A friend told us that he and his girlfriend had broken up, and the cold rain got him down even more. I felt that I had let him down because I hadn't noticed earlier that he was sad.
[Normal] On Sunday my alarm went off at six, and I woke up in the dark. I tried not to wake up my roommates, so I put on my boots quietly. In the kitchen, our friend heated up the leftover soup, and I filled up my water bottle. We warmed up with some stretching before the walk. The path was muddy, but we decided to carry on. In the afternoon we checked out of the hostel and took the bus back to Bogotá.
[Normal] Lectura 6 · The Book That Changed My Mind
[Normal] In my second year I had a problem: I read only because my professors told me to. One rainy afternoon I went to the library to look for a grammar book, but instead I came across a small yellow book about education. I picked out that book almost by chance. Its first pages said that we read the world before we read words. It was an idea of Paulo Freire, the Brazilian educator.
[Normal] I dived into the book until the library closed. Then I wanted to find out more, so I checked out the library computer to see what else Freire and other educators had written. That night I went through my notes twice and could not sleep.
[Normal] The next day I brought up Freire in class, and my classmates listened with interest. I needed time to think over everything, but one thing was clear: reading was not only a duty, it was a door. Since then I believe in the power of one good book to change a student's life.
[Normal] Lectura 7 · A History of Ideas
[Normal] On Tuesdays we had History of Pedagogy. One morning the bus was late and I had to hurry up to class. I tried to get in quietly, but the door was noisy and everyone turned around. The professor only smiled. She had already set up the projector.
[Normal] She turned on the lights and began a story that traveled from ancient Greece to the modern school. She told us about Moacir Gadotti, a Brazilian educator who worked closely with Paulo Freire and wrote about the history of pedagogical ideas. At first she spoke so quickly that I could not keep up with her, so a student asked her to slow down.
[Normal] When we reached Comenius and his dream of teaching everything to everyone, the discussion began to speed up, because everybody wanted to talk. The professor brought out an old school notebook from the 1950s and passed it around. Then she asked each group to look into one pedagogue and present the next week. At the end she turned off the projector and said, “Every idea was once a student's question.”
[Normal] Lectura 8 · Learning by Doing
[Normal] In my fourth semester I had to take on my first teaching practice in a public school in Bogotá. John Dewey said that we learn by doing, and now I had to do it. I had planned a listening game, but when I arrived the children were very noisy. For a moment I wanted to chicken out and go home, but the class was waiting.
[Normal] I took a breath and asked the children to calm down. Then the speaker broke down in the middle of my activity. I had to deal with the problem alone, so I decided to press on and sing the song myself. The children laughed and sang with me. When their teacher returned, she backed me up in front of the principal and said that a plan can fail while a class succeeds.
[Normal] I carried out the rest of the lesson with paper and my own voice. At the end I picked up the papers from the floor, and that night I threw away my first plan and wrote a better one. I learned that a teacher also learns by doing.
[Normal] Lectura 9 · The Zone of Proximal Development
[Normal] In Learning Theories class we studied Vygotsky, who said that people learn better with the help of others. Daniel and I get along very well, so we decided to test his idea. Every Thursday we met up with Laura, a shy classmate who was failing grammar and wanted to give up. We tried to cheer up Laura with jokes and small victories.
[Normal] Laura could not figure out the past perfect, so Daniel suggested, “Let's do it over, but this time together.” Then we came up with a card game to practice the verbs. On Saturdays we hung out at the library cafeteria, and before the exam Laura stayed over at my house. A month later she had caught up with the rest of the class. She did not need us anymore, and that was the best part.
[Normal] Lectura 10 · Little Scientists
[Normal] Every Wednesday I got on a bus and got off near a small primary school. A father dropped off his son at the gate every morning, and I looked after the youngest group until class began. Piaget believed that children build knowledge by exploring, so my job was to care for them and give them questions, not only answers.
[Normal] For the end-of-year festival, the children prepared a play about scientists. A boy tried on a huge white coat, and a girl put on paper glasses. They wanted to dress up as Marie Curie and Newton, and they argued about who would be who. On Fridays they could dress down and wear their favorite clothes, but on the day of the play everyone looked like a real scientist. When the show ended, they took off their costumes slowly, as if they did not want the day to finish.
[Normal] Lectura 11 · The Student Who Never Spoke
[Normal] At my practice school there was a boy named Julián who never spoke in English class. Other children butted in whenever he tried, and he believed that his English was bad. That really got him down, and I did not want to let him down. One day I decided to ask about his hobbies, and his eyes lit up: he loved football.
[Normal] Bruner called it scaffolding: the teacher gives temporary help until the student can walk alone. So I made a rule: nobody could laugh at mistakes, and I would not put up with anyone who did. We warmed up with a song, and then Julián made up a funny story about a dragon who played football. He spoke slowly, with a small voice, but he spoke.
[Normal] “Hang on, teacher! I have one more story,” he said when the bell rang. The class clapped, and somebody asked me to turn up the music so we could celebrate. The following week I proposed a storytelling club, and the principal did not turn down the idea. She approved it.
[Normal] Lectura 12 · The Circle of Dialogue
[Normal] Every Friday afternoon, a small group got together in an old classroom for what we called the Circle of Dialogue. Students from any semester could come along, and some professors sometimes dropped by just to listen. There was one rule: nobody had the last word. Paulo Freire defended dialogue in education, and we wanted to practice it.
[Normal] One night a first-year student asked how the idea of the school itself came about, and the question started a two-hour conversation about history, power, childhood and hope. Nobody looked at their phones. Nobody wanted to leave.
[Normal] Some people wanted to stay in the circle until midnight, while others went out to buy hot chocolate for everyone. Before leaving, a girl said to me, “Why don't you come over tomorrow? My family has a library full of old books.” That is how the circle grew: one invitation at a time.
[Normal] Lectura 13 · A Letter to a Future Teacher
[Normal] Dear first-year student,
[Normal] When I began this degree, I was afraid that I did not belong here. Now, near the end, I want to tell you a few things I learned. Every morning I get up before the sun and ask myself why I want to teach. Write that reason down, and never put off your dreams for a better moment.
[Normal] In my first presentation I almost passed out from nerves, but I carried on, and those five minutes taught me more than a whole book. Watch out for the voice that says you are not good enough. Cut down on comparing yourself with others; each teacher grows at a different speed. When you feel lost, go back to your books, to your notes and to the reason you began.
[Normal] Give away your knowledge generously: a teacher who shares becomes richer. One day you will take over a class of your own and tidy up the room before the children arrive, and you will understand that teaching is an act of hope.
[Normal] With all my heart, Camila
[Normal] Lectura 14 · The Teacher Who Dared
[Normal] One October afternoon, a visiting teacher named Marta spoke to us at the university. She works in a small rural school with no library and fifty children. “At the beginning I had to get by with a very small salary,” she said, “but I wanted my students to read.”
[Normal] She decided to save up for books. Every month she put aside a little money, and she had to cut back on her own expenses. A neighbor lent her some money, and later she managed to pay back him. Years later she was able to pay off the small loan she took to build a shelf. She put the books in a wooden box, and every morning she took it out of the closet and opened it in the middle of the classroom.
[Normal] “When the rain was too strong, I did not call off the class,” Marta said. “I picked up the smallest children at their houses myself.” She told us that her school tries to follow the idea of a citizen school, an idea linked to Moacir Gadotti, where students learn to take part in their community. When she finished, nobody moved. We understood that a teacher with few resources can still open worlds.
[Normal] Lectura 15 · Graduation Day
[Normal] Graduation day finally arrived. My parents had traveled from another city, and they checked in at a small hotel the night before. I woke up before my alarm went off, so I decided to work out for twenty minutes to calm my nerves. Then I tried not to wake up my little brother, who was sleeping on the sofa, and my dad heated up the leftover soup for an early breakfast. We all gobbled up it in five minutes.
[Normal] At the ceremony I saw my whole journey: the rainy afternoon in the library, the practice school, the Circle of Dialogue. A classmate asked out the girl he liked, and she said yes in front of everyone. A couple of friends had broken up during the degree, but they hugged each other like old friends.
[Normal] The next day my parents had to check out early, and their plane took off at noon. I watched it go and felt sad but very proud. I was not only a student anymore. I was a teacher, and the learning had just begun.
